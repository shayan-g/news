Anthropic9/10

Anthropic files to go public as ANT — and Wall Street expects the largest IPO ever

Why it matters: The S-1 filed with the SEC this morning opens the books: $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026 against $4.6 billion for all of 2025, an $80.7 billion net loss of which roughly $72 billion is non-cash charges on convertible instruments, nearly $9 billion of first-half compute spending, and up to $750 billion in potential compute commitments. About a fifth of revenue comes from just two customers. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead; the listing is Nasdaq under "ANT"; Reuters has reported a raise of as much as $100 billion at a roughly $2 trillion valuation — four times Aramco's record. Anthropic would go public as a public benefit corporation, a first at this scale.

What’s next: Reports point to a mid-November offering, possibly the week of November 16 — subject to the SEC process and to whatever this week's markets decide to be.

Science & Space7/10

Japan is going to Phobos — to bring back the first piece of the Mars system

Why it matters: MMX launched October 20 from Tanegashima on the H3's first interplanetary flight, after a two-year delay from the rocket's failed debut. The plan: reach Mars in August 2027, land on Phobos, drill at least 10 grams from below the surface, drop a French-German rover (IDEFIX, ~100 days of driving) and return the sample by 2031 — which would beat every Mars sample-return effort to the punch by riding home from a moon instead of a gravity well.

What’s next: Cruise checkouts now; Mars orbit insertion August 2027.

SpaceX will try to catch both Starship stages next flight — full orbital reuse, finally on the line

Why it matters: On SpaceX's October 20 earnings call, Musk said Flight 15 — no earlier than November 8 — will attempt the first tower catch of the Starship upper stage alongside the now-routine booster catch (three since October 2024). Six months in orbit doesn't disqualify a human heart from Mars

Why it matters: A Circulation study of 13 ISS astronauts found hearts shrink and weaken in the first two weeks of flight — then recover toward baseline as missions continue, provided the exercise regimen holds.
