Hormuz is writing the Fed’s next move, and the bond market is reading it first

Oil is doing the work inflation data no longer can. Brent crude settled at $97.46 after a 14% September run driven by Strait of Hormuz disruption, and that is why a genuinely cooler August PCE inflation print — 3.4% headline, down from 3.7% — bought the bond market nothing: the 10-year Treasury yield still pushed through 5.30%, its highest since 2002, with traders now pricing back-to-back hikes on Oct. 28 and in December. September’s tape shows exactly who pays for that, the Dow down 4.3% on the month while the Nasdaq rose 1.9%. The geopolitical ledger moved the same day in two directions: Washington closed out 23 years in Iraq with the last troops out of Erbil, while Moscow handed NATO a note threatening a nuclear response to any blockade of Kaliningrad. And the capital that is not waiting on any of this kept spending — Gemini 4 Argon arrived as Google’s new frontier model, and a Google AI satellite goes up today to test whether inference belongs in orbit at all.

The last US troops leave Iraq, closing a 23-year military presence

Why it matters: The final American forces and equipment flew out of Erbil Air Base on Wednesday, ending Operation Inherent Resolve in Iraq and a presence that began with the 2003 invasion. The Pentagon says it will keep supplying training and intelligence, so the relationship becomes a conventional bilateral security arrangement rather than a coalition deployment.

What’s next: The withdrawal fulfills a September 2024 agreement with Baghdad; the open question is whether Iraqi forces can hold ground against Islamic State remnants without US air and ISR support.

SCIENCE7/10

Enceladus may hand a future probe its biosignatures pre-concentrated

Why it matters: Two Freie Universität Berlin studies in Science Advances show that as Enceladus plume ice grains form and fragment, ocean components separate and concentrate into individual particles. A probe would not need to average the plume — it would need to find one grain, which existing instruments could read.

What’s next: The finding feeds directly into instrument design for ESA’s planned L4 mission to the moon.

The coolest known lava world is still holding onto an atmosphere

Why it matters: JWST mid-infrared eclipse measurements of HD 3167 b, a rocky super-Earth 154 light-years away with a one-day year, show a dayside cooler than a bare rock should be — evidence of an atmosphere on the smallest lava world found so far. Composition is still unknown.

What’s next: Follow-up spectroscopy would test for heavier gases such as CO2, CO or water.

103 genomes and 44 fossils move the origin of bats to Europe

Why it matters: The the Bat1K consortium consortium — 137 researchers, 64 countries — combined 103 genomes covering all 21 bat families with 44 fossils and put the group’s origin in Europe about 65 to 60 million years ago, displacing Asian and African hypotheses. Placing the fossil Vielasia on the oldest branch also pushes echolocation back to near the dawn of bat evolution.
